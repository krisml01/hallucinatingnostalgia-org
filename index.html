hallucinating nostalgia

/həˌluːsɪˈneɪtɪŋ nɒˈstældʒə/ practice

The deliberate inducing of nostalgia for a time one never lived. One takes an artefact of that time, functioning or restorable, a vinyl record or a film camera perhaps; operating it is the dose. The hallucination comes on with use: one goes back to a time one was never in, and re-enacts a memory one never had.

Ordinary nostalgia is for a past one lived, and it tends to arrive uninvited. Hallucinatory nostalgia is for a past one never lived, and one brings it on. The agency is the difference.

The practice is pragmatic, productive devolution: making a better present out of elements of the past, not out of the past itself. The connection runs through a kind of experimental archaeology: functioning artefacts as history books one can operate.

The stance is an optimistic disillusionment. The memory isn't one's own and the picture may not be accurate; the technology and the access are real, and one trusts the pull.

hallucinatory nostalgia

/həˈluːsɪnəˌtɔːri nɒˈstældʒə/ practice, the concept; open to anyone

The same practice, named rather than done. The adjective describes it; the agent performs it.

origin

alucinari Latin, to wander in the mind. Gives hallucinate.

nostos Greek, homecoming.

algos Greek, pain. With nostos gives nostalgia, coined by Johannes Hofer in 1688 as a disease of the imagination.

related
anemoia (Koenig, 2013); historical nostalgia (Stern, 1992); vicarious nostalgia (Goulding, 2002); hauntology (Fisher, 2014). None names the practice.
